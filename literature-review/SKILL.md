# Literature Review

## Why this matters

A related-work section that just lists papers ("A did X. B did Y. C did
Z.") wastes the reader's time and, worse, fails to do the one thing this
section exists for: showing exactly where the gap is that justifies the
new work. Reviewers read this section looking for that gap — if they can't
find it, they'll assume it doesn't exist.

## Process

1. **Clarify the review's purpose before searching.** A literature review
   for a related-work section in a paper is scoped tightly around the
   paper's specific contribution. A standalone survey is scoped broadly
   across a whole subfield. A background section for a grant proposal is
   scoped around justifying why the funded work matters. Ask which one if
   it's unclear — the scope changes what counts as relevant.

2. **Search broadly, then filter deliberately.** Cast a wide net (multiple
   search engines/databases, varied search terms including synonyms and
   adjacent terminology different subfields use for the same idea), then
   filter down based on relevance to the specific question, not just
   citation count or recency. A highly-cited paper that's tangential is
   less useful here than a small paper that's directly on point.

3. **For each source, capture more than the citation:**
   - What specifically did they do (method/approach)?
   - What did they find/claim?
   - How does it relate to the current work — same problem different
     method, same method different domain, a finding this work builds on,
     a finding this work contradicts or extends?
   - Track this in a structured way (a table works well) so synthesis
     later doesn't require re-reading everything.

4. **Organize by theme or argument, not by chronology or by paper.**
   Group sources around the ideas they contribute to, and build the
   section as an argument that moves from broad context to the specific
   gap. "Paper A did X (2019). Then paper B did Y (2021)." is a list.
   "Prior approaches to this problem fall into two families: [family 1,
   with its limitation] and [family 2, with its limitation]. Neither
   addresses [the specific gap]." is a synthesis.

5. **State the gap explicitly and make it earn its place.** The gap should
   be a real limitation of prior work relative to the question at hand —
   not a strawman ("no one has tried X" when X was actually tried, just
   under a different name) and not trivial ("no one has done this exact
   combination" without explaining why that combination matters).

6. **Cite accurately.** Don't attribute a claim to a paper that only
   implies it or attribute a finding more broadly than the paper actually
   supports. Misrepresenting prior work is one of the fastest ways to lose
   a reviewer's trust, and it's usually caught.

## Structure for a related-work / literature-review section

- **Opening**: what area of prior work is relevant and why (one or two
  sentences of framing).
- **Thematic groups**: organize prior work into 2–4 meaningful clusters,
  each with a clear throughline, not one paragraph per paper.
- **Synthesis, not summary**: within each cluster, say what's shared and
  what differs across the works, not just describe each one in sequence.
- **The gap**: explicitly connect the limitations just discussed to what
  the current work does differently or newly.
- **Positioning statement**: a sentence that states, plainly, how this work
  relates to and differs from what came before.

## Citation and bibliography organization

When the user is managing a working set of references (not yet writing
prose), organize them as a table or structured list with: citation key,
short description of contribution, relevance to the current project, and
any direct quote or specific number worth preserving verbatim (page-
referenced if possible, since exact wording matters if it'll be quoted).
This turns literature review into reusable notes rather than one-off
reading.
