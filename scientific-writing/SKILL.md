# Scientific Writing

## Why this matters

Good science can still fail to land because the writing hides it: buried
findings, hedged claims, sentences that make the reader do the work of
finding the subject and verb. The goal here is not decoration — it's making
sure a busy reader (or a skeptical reviewer) gets the point on the first
pass. Every suggestion below exists to reduce the distance between what the
author knows and what the reader understands.

## Core craft principles

Apply these when drafting or editing any scientific text, regardless of
target venue:

- **One idea per sentence, one claim per paragraph.** If a sentence needs
  three commas and two "which"-clauses to hold together, it's carrying too
  much. Split it.
- **Put the actor before the action.** Passive voice ("was observed that")
  is not wrong, but it's often used to avoid saying who did what or to
  soften a claim. Default to active voice ("we observed") unless the actor
  is genuinely irrelevant (e.g., describing a general mechanism).
- **Old information first, new information last.** Readers parse a sentence
  faster when it opens with something already established and ends with the
  new point. Chaining paragraphs this way (the last idea of one sentence
  becomes the subject of the next) is what makes a paragraph feel like an
  argument instead of a list.
- **Front-load the claim, then support it.** Topic sentences should state
  the point of the paragraph, not tease it. Save suspense for fiction.
- **Cut nominalizations.** "We performed an analysis of" → "we analyzed."
  "The generation of X was achieved by" → "we generated X." Turning verbs
  into nouns is the single most common way scientific writing gets bloated.
- **Quantify instead of hedging vaguely.** "Significantly higher" without a
  number or a stats reference is weaker than "2.3-fold higher (p = 0.002)."
  If a claim can carry a number, give it one.
- **Match confidence to evidence.** Don't hedge findings that are solid
  ("may possibly suggest a potential trend") and don't overstate findings
  that aren't ("proves"). Reviewers notice both, and both cost trust.
- **Kill jargon that isn't load-bearing.** Technical terms are fine when
  they're precise and necessary; they're a liability when they're just
  field habit. If a term can be replaced by a plain-English phrase without
  losing precision, replace it.
- **Read it aloud (or have the model do a pass simulating that).** Sentences
  that are hard to say tend to be hard to parse. This catches more problems
  than a silent re-read.

## Structural checklist

Use IMRaD (Introduction, Methods, Results, Discussion) as the default
skeleton for a research paper, and check that each section is doing its
actual job rather than drifting into another section's territory:

- **Title** — states the finding, not just the topic. "X regulates Y" beats
  "Investigations into the role of X." Cut filler words ("a study of," "an
  analysis of," "novel," "the role of").
- **Abstract** — should work as a 4–6 sentence stand-alone argument:
  context → gap/question → what was done → what was found → why it
  matters. Every sentence should be answerable without reading the rest of
  the paper.
- **Introduction** — narrows from the broad problem to the specific gap
  this paper fills, and ends by stating what the paper does about it. If a
  reader can't find the gap in the first page, restructure.
- **Results** — organized around the story of the findings, not the
  chronology of experiments. Each subsection should open with the question
  it answers, not with methodology.
- **Discussion** — interprets, doesn't repeat. It should say what the
  results mean, how they relate to prior work, what the limitations are,
  and what follows — not restate the Results section in past tense.
- **Figure legends** — self-contained enough that a reader flipping
  straight to the figure understands what's shown, what the axes/colors
  mean, and what the punchline is, without hunting through the main text.

## Editing pass

When asked to revise existing text rather than draft from scratch:

1. Read the whole passage once before touching anything — don't fix line 1
   before you know what line 20 is trying to say.
2. Identify the actual claim of each paragraph and check the topic sentence
   states it.
3. Apply the craft principles above sentence by sentence.
4. Aim to cut length, not just rearrange it — a common failure mode of
   "editing" is making prose longer by hedging or over-explaining. Dense
   and short beats padded and long.
5. Flag (rather than silently fix) anything where the intended meaning is
   ambiguous — don't guess at unstated science.

## Journal-specific conventions

If the user names a specific journal (Nature, Science, Cell, PNAS, etc.) or
is preparing a submission, read `references/nature-conventions.md` for
detailed, checkable conventions (word limits, section structure, title and
cover-letter norms, common rejection triggers). Nature's conventions are
also a reasonable default model for "high-impact generalist journal" style
even when the user hasn't named Nature specifically, since PNAS/Science/Cell
share most of the same norms (broad-audience accessibility, front-loaded
significance, strict length discipline).
