# Nature-style submission conventions

These are the conventions that distinguish Nature-family journals (Nature,
Nature Communications, Nature Medicine, etc.) and are close enough to
Science/Cell/PNAS that they're a reasonable default for any high-impact
generalist journal. Always check the specific target journal's current
author guidelines for exact numbers — the values below are the well-known
ballpark figures, not a substitute for the journal's own instructions page,
which changes over time.

## Overall editorial tone

Nature is written for a broad scientific readership, not just specialists
in the subfield. A cell biologist and a physicist should both be able to
follow the abstract and understand why the finding matters, even if the
methodological details are outside their expertise. This means:

- Explain the significance before the mechanism.
- Define field-specific abbreviations and jargon on first use, or avoid
  them entirely if a plain term works.
- Open with the broader question the work addresses, not the specific
  assay or model system.

## Typical length limits (research Articles)

- Main text: roughly 2,000–3,000 words (varies by journal/article type —
  check current guidelines), excluding abstract, methods, references,
  and figure legends.
- Abstract: ~150 words, written without citations or abbreviations,
  understandable on its own.
- Title: short (often a hard character limit, e.g. ~75–90 characters),
  states the finding as a claim, not a topic. No abbreviations, no
  punctuation tricks, no question marks in most cases.
- Figures: a limited number of display items (commonly 4–8 for a research
  article) — figures need to carry a lot of information density because
  there are few of them.
- References: often capped (e.g., ~30–50 for a research article),
  formatted in Nature's numbered citation style.

## Section structure

Nature research articles typically do **not** use explicit
"Introduction/Results/Discussion" subheadings within the main text — the
narrative flows continuously, though it still follows an IMRaD logic
underneath:

1. Unheaded opening (functions as Introduction) — states the broad context,
   the specific gap, and what this paper shows, in a few short paragraphs.
2. Unheaded body (functions as Results, often with bolded run-in
   sub-topics rather than full subheadings) — presents findings in a
   narrative order driven by the logic of the argument, not the
   chronological order experiments were run.
3. A short closing paragraph or two (functions as Discussion) — situates
   the finding in the broader field and states significance/implications,
   without repeating the results in detail.
4. **Methods** appear online/after the main text, written densely for
   reproducibility rather than readability — this is where technical
   detail belongs, not in the main narrative.

## Title conventions

- States the finding: "Protein X drives Y in Z" rather than "The role of
  protein X in Y."
- Avoid: "novel," "a study of," "insights into," "the effect of" — these
  are filler that a title doesn't need.
- No unexplained abbreviations.

## Cover letter

A cover letter to a Nature-family editor should, briefly (typically under
one page):

- State the core finding in one or two sentences up front.
- Explain why it's significant *for this journal's broad readership*
  specifically — not just why it matters to the subfield. This is the
  single most common weak point: authors explain the finding but not why
  a generalist journal's editor should care.
- Note what's new relative to prior work (briefly — this isn't the
  Discussion section restated).
- Confirm anything procedurally required (not under consideration
  elsewhere, all authors approve, etc., per the specific journal's
  requirements).

## Common reasons editors reject before review

Useful as a self-check before submission:

- The significance is stated for the subfield but not for a general
  reader — editors reject a lot of technically sound work for "insufficient
  general interest" when the framing fails to make the broader case.
- The abstract undersells or buries the actual finding.
- The claim outruns the evidence (overstated generality, causal language
  for correlational data).
- Length or figure-count limits are ignored, signaling the manuscript
  wasn't tailored to this journal.
- The title is descriptive of a topic rather than a claim, making it hard
  for an editor to tell in five seconds what was found.
